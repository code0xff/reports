# Behavior Control Techniques for AI Agent Payments

## Abstract

*Agentic payments* — where an AI agent completes a purchase without a human's real-time click — are standardizing fast. This report approaches them not from "how to let an agent pay" but from **"how to control an agent's payment behavior"**, and organizes the implementable techniques into four layers: (A) cryptographic / credential-level intent and scope constraints, (B) policy and spending-limit engines, (C) agent-runtime guardrails and human-in-the-loop, and (D) verification, attribution, and audit. Three conclusions stand out. First, the behavior to be controlled decomposes into dimensions — *amount, recipient, category, frequency, time, and approving party* — and commercial infrastructure offers controls along exactly these axes[^s10][^s29][^s33]. Second, the techniques differ in enforcement strength: cryptographically signed, scope-limited authority (AP2 Mandates, x402 exact/upto, ERC-7579 session keys) is enforced most strongly, whereas LLM-side guardrails are probabilistic and bypassable[^s06][^s24][^s16][^s41]. Third, *defense-in-depth* is therefore required rather than any single technique — the fact that prompt injection has no fool-proof defense is the crux of this conclusion[^s41][^s39][^s40]. This is a June 2026 snapshot; many of the standards involved are still drafts or betas, which we state as a limitation.

## 1. Introduction — What, and Why, to Control

Agentic payment refers to a flow in which an AI agent, given a delegated goal, autonomously discovers goods or services and completes payment. Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) extends this to "Human Not Present" payments, specifying scenarios in which, once a price reaches a user-set condition, the agent completes the purchase with pre-authorized credentials and mandates without asking for real-time confirmation[^s04].

This autonomy makes control hard for two reasons that distinguish it from traditional payment automation (subscriptions, standing orders): LLM **non-determinism** and **prompt injection**. The OWASP Gen AI Security Project states that "given the stochastic influence at the heart of the way models work, it is unclear if there are fool-proof methods of prevention for prompt injection"[^s41]. The threat is not hypothetical: in a 2026 case an AI wallet on X was prompt-injected and drained of roughly $150,000, with analysis concluding the failure point was "intent parsing," not blockchain infrastructure[^s40]. A red-team study of AP2 likewise reports that "simple adversarial prompts can reliably subvert agent behavior"[^s39].

Control therefore begins by decomposing *what* is being controlled. Synthesizing the commercial documentation, the controllable behavior dimensions are roughly **amount (per-transaction / periodic limits), recipient/merchant, category (e.g. MCC), frequency/velocity, time (expiry/period), and approving party**. Stripe Issuing states you can "block merchant categories, countries, merchant IDs, or card presence, and set spending limits per authorization or per month"[^s29], and Lithic adds MCC/country/currency/risk-score rules plus a velocity limit that "restricts the number or value of transactions over a time period"[^s33]. This report analyzes the mechanisms that enforce these dimensions across four layers.

## 2. Background — Threat Model and a Taxonomy of Control Layers

An agent-payment pipeline flows roughly *user → agent → payment credential → settlement network*, with a different attack surface at each stage. Academic systematization is emerging: "SoK: Blockchain agent-to-agent payments" systematizes blockchain A2A payments such as x402 into a four-stage lifecycle[^s37]. "Five Attacks on x402" presents five concrete attacks exposing weaknesses "in authorization, binding, replay protection, and web-layer handling," showing the protocol is vulnerable across multiple stages of the payment workflow[^s36]. Marino and Juels warn that giving AI agents access to cryptocurrency and smart contracts "could lead to formidable new vectors of AI harm"[^s38].

In trust-boundary terms, the core problem is the limit of the **single-key, single-trust model**. Alqithami notes that a single EOA (externally owned account) controlled by an autonomous agent is "a single point of failure for the funds it controls"[^s48]. Hence, instead of one all-powerful key, designs are encouraged to split authority via *delegation* and *scope* — the starting point of Layer A.

Control techniques can be grouped into four layers by where and how strongly they are enforced:

- **Layer A — Cryptographic / credential**: sign and scope the authority itself by amount, recipient, expiry (strongest enforcement).
- **Layer B — Policy / limit engines**: evaluate limits, allowlists, and velocity rules and block violations.
- **Layer C — Runtime guardrails / HITL**: gate the agent's tool calls and insert a human at thresholds (probabilistic, bypassable).
- **Layer D — Verification / attribution / audit**: verify identity and delegation chains, attribute responsibility, and audit after the fact.

## 3. Layer A — Cryptographic & Credential-Level Constraints

The strongest control is to make the authority itself a *signed, scope-limited object*. Even if the agent tries to exceed that scope, a verifier checks the signature and scope, so the payment does not go through.

**Signed intent / delegation (Mandate/Intent).** AP2's core design fits here. AP2 chains three cryptographically signed mandates — an *Intent Mandate* capturing the user's intent, a *Cart Mandate* approving a specific cart at a specific price, and a *Payment Mandate* — and signs them as Verifiable Credentials forming a tamper-proof audit trail[^s02]. The Intent Mandate model constrains scope at the code level with fields like `merchants`, `skus`, `requires_refundability`, and `intent_expiry`[^s03]. The Payment Mandate is bound to a particular checkout via the cryptographic hash of the Checkout JWT, so it cannot be reused for another payment[^s01]. The card networks are similar[^s07]. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol says it "employs cryptographic message signatures that are specific to the merchant and purpose, and are time bound, cannot be replayed or relayed"[^s08] _(vendor-stated)_, and Mastercard Agent Pay uses tokenization to mask identifying information during transactions[^s09].

**Scoped delegation / session keys.** The on-chain smart-account ecosystem implements the same idea as modules. ERC-4337 (account abstraction), EIP-7702 (set code for EOAs), ERC-7579 (modular smart accounts), and ERC-7710 (smart-contract delegation) form the base[^s12][^s13][^s14][^s15], on top of which the SmartSessions module provides "granular control over session keys ... various policy types: user-operation validation, action-specific policies"[^s16]. ZeroDev gives a session key "specific permissions (which contracts, which functions, what amounts) and a time limit," so it can sign only transactions matching those permissions[^s19], and Biconomy calls this "powerful guardrails for signers — primarily bots or AI agents"[^s20]. The MetaMask Delegation Toolkit uses ERC-7710/7715 to "assign limited execution permissions so AI agents can trade on your behalf"[^s17], and its spending-limit scopes internally use the `erc20Streaming` and `valueLte` caveat enforcers[^s18]. Coinbase's Spend Permission structure expresses the delegation scope with `account, spender, token, allowance, period, start, end` fields[^s21].

**Amount-capped one-time authority.** On-chain payment schemes turn authority into a one-time signature narrowed to a *specific amount cap, recipient, and expiry*. x402's `exact` scheme has the facilitator pay gas while "the client (user) controls the exact flow of funds via cryptographic signatures"[^s23], and the `upto` scheme mandates that "each authorization MUST be settled at most once; after settlement the authorization is consumed and cannot be reused"[^s24]. This authority is implemented via EIP-3009 (signature-based transfer authorization)[^s26] or Permit2 (one-time signature transfer without long-lived allowances)[^s27], and Coinbase's x402 docs state payments can be made "via EIP-3009 or Permit2"[^s25]. Bridging card and machine rails, Stripe's Shared Payment Token (SPT) "grants the seller scoped access to the customer's payment method" with usage limits "including currency, maximum amount, and expiration window"[^s30].

A caveat applies to enforcement strength, though. Contrary to x402 being marketed as the "internet-native payment standard"[^s25], "Five Attacks on x402" demonstrated concrete attacks on the same protocol's authorization, binding, and replay protection[^s36] — both sides are presented to show that even a cryptographic scheme loses its enforcement strength under design or implementation flaws.

## 4. Layer B — Policy & Spending-Limit Engines

If credentials enforce "what was signed," policy engines evaluate "does this payment comply with the rules" and block it otherwise. Commercial infrastructure offers this control as an explicit feature.

**Spending limits and velocity.** Stripe Issuing supports per-authorization and monthly spending limits[^s29], and Lithic offers a single per-card limit (annual/forever/monthly/per-transaction) plus multiple concurrent account-level limits (daily/monthly/lifetime), with a velocity limit that "restricts the number or value of transactions over a time period"[^s33][^s32]. On-chain, Crossmint provides "per-transaction limits (cap any single payment), rolling caps (limit total spend over a time window like 24 hours), and recipient allowlists (restrict which addresses the agent can pay)"[^s34].

**Merchant / category constraints.** Stripe Issuing can block merchant categories, countries, and merchant IDs, and advises that "spending limits alone don't block categories and should be used with either `allowed_categories` or `blocked_categories` to restrict spending to specific business types"[^s29]. Lithic enforces rules based on MCC, country, currency, and risk score[^s33].

**Where it is enforced: on-chain validator vs off-chain engine.** Where policy is enforced governs its strength. On-chain policy modules (SmartSessions' policy types, MetaMask's caveat enforcers) are enforced directly by the smart contract at transaction-validation time, making them harder to bypass than an off-chain policy server[^s16][^s18]. An off-chain policy engine (Privy, for instance, explains that "policies define the boundaries within which your agents can operate") leads on expressiveness and flexibility, but application-logic limits can be bypassed by the agent, a compromised prompt, or a bug, making the enforcement point weaker[^s22] _(interpretive)_. Coinbase documents that "spend permissions allow smart accounts to delegate spending authority to other accounts within specified limits and time periods"[^s50]. This is not absolute, however — even an on-chain validator will pass *a compromised session key's spending as legitimate within its scope*, so the limit itself becomes the ceiling on loss _(interpretive)_. Acharya's work addresses verifying authenticity and intent in a trustless environment, but the body is available only as a PDF, so direct quotation is withheld[^s49].

**Card-network-level enforcement.** The card camp binds constraints to the issued token and the network. Visa Intelligent Commerce offers "spending limits, approval workflows, authentication requirements and trusted identity signals"[^s10], and its successor Intelligent Commerce Connect is reported to let "users preset dollar limits, merchant categories or real-time approval prompts — guardrails that load directly into VisaNet"[^s11] _(vendor-stated, single report)_.

## 5. Layer C — Runtime Guardrails and Human-in-the-Loop

Where the first two layers control payment *authority*, this layer controls the *agent that triggers* the payment. Since payments usually begin as an LLM tool call, gating tool calls is the first line of defense.

**Guardrail frameworks.** The OpenAI Agents SDK states that "if the guardrail tripwire is triggered, the agent never executes, preventing token consumption and tool execution"[^s42]. NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails' execution rails gate the tools/actions an LLM application can call[^s43], and Guardrails AI automatically runs input validators before the call and output validators after[^s44]. Including payment tools among these validated actions lets a policy-violating payment call be blocked before execution.

**Prompt-injection defense as a precondition.** Guardrails are probabilistic, however. OWASP states there may be no fool-proof defense against prompt injection and recommends "human-in-the-loop controls for privileged operations to prevent unauthorized actions"[^s41]. Payment is a textbook privileged operation. Treating external input as *data, not instruction* is the precondition for payment control; violating it is how the intent-parsing stage was breached in the Grok wallet case[^s40]. Anthropic notes that "Claude already uses a system of classifiers to detect and guard against misuses such as prompt injections, in addition to several other layers of security"[^s46].

**Human-in-the-loop / step-up.** Inserting a human for over-threshold payments is recommended by both standards and frameworks. AP2's Human Present flow has the user directly approve closed Checkout and Payment Mandates, and even a Human Not Present flow can be turned back into Human Present when the merchant or credential provider returns an `unresolved_constraint` error[^s04]. LangChain's HITL middleware "adds human oversight to agent tool calls and issues an interrupt that halts execution" when intervention is needed[^s45]. The Skyfire case — where "once an agent hits a particular size transaction, it can send the user an SMS for approval and then continue" — is the same pattern[^s35]. Anthropic states as a principle that "humans should retain control over how their goals are pursued, particularly before high-stakes decisions are made"[^s46].

**Isolating non-determinism.** Finally, separating the payment's key parameters from the LLM's natural-language output into a *deterministic signed object* reduces non-determinism risk. AP2 explains that "trust in payments is anchored to deterministic, non-repudiable proof of intent from the user, directly addressing the risk of agent error or hallucination"[^s06][^s02].

## 6. Layer D — Verification, Attribution & Audit

The final layer is the after-the-fact and cross-cutting control that *verifies, records, and attributes* whether control worked.

**Verifiable identity / authority.** Delegation-chain control requires being able to verify who the agent is and what authority it was delegated. ERC-8004 (Trustless Agents) proposes using blockchains "to discover, choose, and interact with agents across organizational boundaries without pre-existing trust"[^s28]. AP2 also defines two delegation models (User Credential, Trusted Agent Provider) to make explicit which issuer a verifier trusts[^s05].

**Non-repudiation and attribution.** A chain of signed mandates proves after the fact "who approved what." AP2 states it "provides a non-repudiable, cryptographic audit trail for every transaction, aiding in dispute resolution"[^s06] _(vendor-stated)_. This is the basis for attributing responsibility — to the user, agent, or merchant — when a control fails.

**Audit logs / observability.** Complementing real-time control is after-the-fact audit. One engineering guide explains that "by logging every tool call and comparing it against a predefined policy decision (e.g., a guardrail), you create a feedback loop that identifies when an agent attempts to exceed its authority"[^s47]. Anthropic's layered security likewise combines classifiers and monitoring[^s46].

## 7. Analysis & Discussion — Combining Techniques and Trade-offs

**Enforcement-strength spectrum.** The four layers differ in enforcement strength. Cryptographically signed, scope-limited authority (Layer A) and on-chain/network-level policy enforcement (Layer B) are enforced deterministically — an out-of-scope payment fails verification, or the limit becomes the ceiling on loss[^s24][^s16][^s50]. Agent-side guardrails (Layer C), by contrast, depend on the LLM's probabilistic behavior, so, as OWASP states, complete defense is not guaranteed[^s41]. A design that tries to control payments with guardrails alone is therefore structurally fragile — the Grok case and the AP2 red team demonstrate this empirically[^s40][^s39] — and defense-in-depth combining classifiers, HITL, and multiple layers (as Anthropic does) is recommended[^s46].

**Threat → layer mapping (defense-in-depth).** Unauthorized/excess spend is blocked by Layer A's amount-capped authority and Layer B's limits; a wrong merchant by the mandate's `merchants` scope and allowlists[^s03][^s29]; injection-induced payments by Layer C guardrails/HITL as the first line and by Layer A's signed scope as the *last-resort ceiling*. Replay is blocked by one-time authority (x402 upto's "settle at most once," AP2's Checkout-JWT binding)[^s24][^s01], and attribution failure is covered by Layer D's mandate audit trail[^s06]. The key point is that no single layer blocks every threat.

**Differences in where control lives.** The same behavior dimension carries different trust assumptions depending on which stack enforces it _(interpretive)_. On-chain smart accounts place control in the smart contract (decentralized verification)[^s16][^s50]; card networks place it in the issuer/network (VisaNet)[^s11]; the model-provider stack places it in the credential token and the agent runtime[^s30][^s42]. Stripe bridges both, noting "in both card and machine-native flows, the payment credential can be scoped with controls like amount, currency, and merchant"[^s31]. Implementers choose where control lives according to whom they want to trust (chain, issuer, or model provider).

**Trade-offs.** Stronger cryptographic control brings more UX friction (pre-signing, scope setup, key management), and because standards like AP2, x402, Stripe ACP, MPP, and ERC-8004 are all still drafts or betas, interoperability and stability remain immature. An independent comparison likewise notes that "broader card-based implementations are still maturing" and that "production agent systems often need elements of all four" (MPP, ACP, AP2, x402) — i.e., no single protocol comprehensively covers every control scenario[^s51]. Balancing control against autonomy and convenience is the central design decision.

## 8. Limitations

- **Immature, moving standards.** AP2 (v0.2, donated to FIDO in 2026), x402 (v2), Stripe ACP (beta), and ERC-8004 are all changing fast; this is a June 2026 snapshot, and spec changes can alter the detailed control behavior. Real-world *adoption* of these controls is also still being proven — an independent analysis judges that "broader card-based implementations are still maturing"[^s51].
- **Reliance on vendor statements.** Phrases like Visa's "cannot be replayed or relayed," Mastercard's tokenization, and AP2's "tamper-proof / non-repudiable" are issuer/vendor marketing language, not independent audits — marked _(vendor-stated)_ in the text[^s08][^s09][^s06].
- **Single / weak-source spots.** The control-location discussion (§7) and some non-repudiation claims rest on synthesis, and the VisaNet guardrail-loading detail rests on a single report (s11). Acharya's paper (s49) was withheld from direct quotation due to PDF access limits.
- **Caveat on on-chain enforcement.** The claim that on-chain is "harder to bypass" is bounded in the key-compromise scenario — a stolen session key passes as legitimate within its scope.
- **Inherent uncertainty of guardrail efficacy.** That prompt injection has no fool-proof defense (OWASP) means Layer C controls reduce but do not eliminate risk[^s41].
