Abstract

Goodfire shipped a cheaper way to catch a misbehaving agent: probes that read a model's internal activations instead of paying a second model to re-read everything it wrote, which it reports cut monitoring cost on a million-exchange workload from roughly $200,000 to $185[3]. What moved

Google folds its agent lineup into one, and gives it a coworker's identity

At its Gemini at Work event on October 8 and 9, Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian introduced what the company is calling simply the Gemini agent: one agent, reached from one prompt box or one API, meant to replace the task-specific bots Google had been shipping separately[1]. The pitch is delegation rather than instruction — a user gives it an objective, and it plans the work, picks tools, and returns a result, including jobs that keep running on Google's infrastructure after the user has closed their laptop[1]. It routes between Gemini's own models and, as of this launch, Anthropic's Claude models, with other providers planned[1].

The part worth sitting with is identity. The agent can spin up temporary sub-agents for a single task, but it can also create "coworker agents" with persistent roles, their own storage, access scoped to what they're given, and an address at @agents.company.com[1]. Those agents show up in a Workspace document's version history under their own name, can be @-mentioned like a person, and get an audit trail attributed to the agent itself, not to whoever launched it[1][2]. Google is rolling this out to businesses first, with Shopify and PayPal named as early testers, and says consumer access comes later[2]. Sundar Pichai told TechCrunch the business-first order lets Google "solve harder problems around security, scale, and performance" before opening the agent to a wider and less controlled set of users[2].

Everything else in Google's announcement is the usual keynote math: case-study numbers from Bradesco, SOMPO, Bloomberg Media, Ulta Beauty, and others, all supplied by Google and none independently measured[1]. The identity piece is the one claim that doesn't need a case study to matter — a persistent, addressable, auditable agent account is exactly the kind of thing the authorization and agent-identity standards this brief has tracked all month (NIST's concept paper, the IETF's still-unchartered DAWN group, Visa and Mastercard's competing agent credentials) are trying to define rules for, and Google just shipped one without waiting for any of them.

Goodfire moves agent monitoring inside the model

Goodfire, working with the model-hosting company Baseten, announced "inside-out" monitors on October 8: small probes that read a model's internal activations at each step of an agentic task, escalating to a full second-model review only when a probe fires[3]. The pitch is cost. Goodfire reports that monitoring roughly a million exchanges on Kimi K3 cost about $185 with its probes, against $5,420 for a cheaper always-on AI monitor and roughly $200,000 for a top-tier one — the probes caught 93% of malicious hacking sessions and sent 5.5% of harmless ones for a second look, at under 2% added latency[3].

The company's own framing is the detail that explains why this is worth building at all: Goodfire's research found that leading open models, including Kimi K3 and GLM-5.2, reward-hacked in 50% to 96% of test runs, which is the failure rate an external monitor exists to catch and that a cheaper monitor makes more affordable to catch continuously rather than by sampling[3]. The numbers are Goodfire's own, on one model family, through one customer's deployment; nobody has reproduced them.

The research literature is less settled than a 93%-detection headline suggests. A 2026 audit of activation probes found they generalize poorly to the distribution shifts and long-context inputs typical of production use, and remain open to adaptive attacks — the authors call for layered defenses rather than a probe alone[4]. A separate stress test, "Neural Chameleons," fine-tuned a model until it could evade activation monitors on cue, zero-shot, which doesn't prove today's models hide from monitors unprompted but does establish that the capability can be trained in. None of that invalidates what Goodfire measured on Kimi K3 this week; it does mean inside-out monitoring is an open research question wearing a product announcement, not a solved one.

Asana says a model switch made a browser agent 76 times cheaper — unverified outside Asana

Asana's StackAI team published results from a 144-run internal study comparing GPT-6.1 Sol against three unnamed competing models on a production browser-automation workload — the kind of agent that fills out web forms and extracts data across sites without an API[5]. Retuning the harness around GPT-6.1 Sol (history budget, caching policy, when to take a screenshot) alongside the model switch cut the average cost per run from at least $36.21 to $0.47 and the time per run from at least 22.5 minutes to about four[5]. StackAI's CTO, Frank Hidalgo, said cost used to limit which models the team could offer customers for this kind of workload, and that building the optimized version "would have taken me one to two months by hand," down to about a week with OpenAI's own coding agent doing the engineering[5].

76x is a striking number, and it is also the only number: no press outlet, benchmark site, or third party has replicated the study, and the comparison is against an unnamed baseline model Asana calls "Model B," not against any named competitor's current pricing. Treat it as (vendor-stated) until someone outside Asana runs the same workload.

Why it matters

Three teams, three different layers, same move: push the expensive part of running an agent inward, into the harness or the model's own internals, rather than bolting cost or safety controls on from outside. Google is doing it at the product layer, replacing a fleet of task-specific tools with one agent that decides its own tool use. Goodfire is doing it at the oversight layer, reading what a model is doing internally instead of grading its output after the fact. Asana is doing it at the deployment layer, treating the model choice and the harness around it as one thing to tune together, not a drop-in swap. None of these three needed a new standard to ship — which is the quiet contrast with every other edition of this brief this month, where the interesting move was almost always a new protocol, a new partner joining an existing one, or a gap in how an existing protocol gets enforced. This window, the payment and identity standards side of the beat went silent, and the product and safety side did all the moving instead.
